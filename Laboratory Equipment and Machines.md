# Laboratory Equipment and Machines

In wet lab work, understanding the purpose of laboratory equipment and machines is very important. Experiments are not only about following a protocol, adding reagents, and waiting for reactions to happen. Behind every step, there is usually a specific piece of equipment that helps maintain sample conditions, control reaction environments, process samples, detect results, and ensure laboratory safety.

Different machines serve different purposes. Some are used for storing samples and reagents, some are used to protect researchers, while others are used for organizing samples, transferring liquids, mixing, heating, amplifying DNA, separating molecules, or detecting experimental results.

This section introduces common equipment used in Monroe Lab. Becoming familiar with the basic purpose of these machines can help us better understand experimental principles, reduce mistakes, and develop the ability to troubleshoot problems more independently.

---

## 1. Storage and Cold Handling Equipment

Before an experiment begins, many samples and reagents need to be stored at specific temperatures. During experiments, many biological molecules also need to be kept cold to prevent degradation. Therefore, refrigerators, freezers, liquid nitrogen, ice buckets, and cooling blocks are all important tools for maintaining sample stability.

---

### 1.1 4°C Refrigerator

A **4°C refrigerator** is mainly used for short-term storage of temperature-sensitive reagents, samples, and culture materials.

Its purpose is to slow down molecular degradation and reduce the activity of bacteria, enzymes, or other biological processes. However, 4°C does not completely stop biological or chemical reactions.

Common items stored at 4°C include:

- Buffers
- Some enzymes or enzyme mixes, depending on manufacturer instructions
- Antibiotic plates
- Culture media
- Agar plates
- Biological samples for short-term storage

In general, 4°C is suitable for temporary or short-term storage. If a sample needs to be stored for a longer time, or if it is highly sensitive to degradation, it usually needs to be stored at **-20°C** or **-80°C**.

---

### 1.2 -20°C Freezer

A **-20°C freezer** is used to store reagents and samples that need to remain frozen but do not necessarily require ultra-low temperature storage.

Compared with 4°C, -20°C is more suitable for medium- to long-term storage of many molecular biology reagents.

Common items stored at -20°C include:

- DNA samples
- Primers
- dNTPs
- Some enzymes
- Antibiotics
- Certain chemical reagents
- RNA samples for short-term storage

One important thing to remember is that repeated freeze-thaw cycles can affect sample or reagent quality, especially for enzymes, DNA/RNA, and other sensitive reagents. Therefore, reagents are often divided into smaller aliquots to reduce repeated freezing and thawing.

---

### 1.3 -80°C Freezer

A **-80°C freezer** is used for long-term storage of highly sensitive or easily degraded biological materials.

It provides a much colder and more stable environment than a -20°C freezer, making it suitable for preserving valuable samples over long periods of time.

Common items stored at -80°C include:

- RNA samples
- Protein samples
- Cell stocks
- Bacterial glycerol stocks
- Tissue samples
- Virus samples
- Long-term biological sample archives

Samples stored at -80°C are often valuable and sometimes difficult to replace. Therefore, clear labeling, accurate location records, and proper freezer organization are very important.

Frequent door opening or leaving the freezer open for too long can cause temperature fluctuations, which may affect sample quality.

---

### 1.4 Liquid Nitrogen Tank

A **liquid nitrogen tank** is used to store or transport liquid nitrogen, which is extremely cold, around **-196°C**. Liquid nitrogen is commonly used when samples need to be frozen very quickly or kept at ultra-low temperatures.

Liquid nitrogen is commonly used for:

- Snap-freezing tissue samples
- Preserving cells or biological samples
- Keeping samples extremely cold during transfer
- Cooling tissue before grinding
- Preserving RNA, protein, or metabolite samples before extraction

For example, in plant or animal tissue experiments, fresh tissue may be immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection. This process is called **snap freezing**. Snap freezing helps stop biological activity quickly and preserves RNA, proteins, metabolites, or other molecules before extraction.

Liquid nitrogen is also useful when grinding tissue. Frozen tissue becomes brittle, which makes it easier to grind into powder and helps prevent molecular degradation during sample preparation.

However, liquid nitrogen is hazardous and must be handled carefully.

Important safety points:

- Liquid nitrogen can cause severe cold burns or frostbite.
- Always wear proper PPE, such as cryogenic gloves, lab coat, and eye/face protection.
- Do not seal liquid nitrogen in a closed container because pressure can build up and cause an explosion.
- Use liquid nitrogen only in well-ventilated areas because nitrogen gas can displace oxygen.
- Be careful when pouring or transferring liquid nitrogen.
- Do not touch liquid nitrogen-cooled metal or surfaces with bare hands.

The main purpose of a liquid nitrogen tank is:

> To store liquid nitrogen for ultra-low-temperature freezing, preservation, and sample handling.

---

### 1.5 Ice Bucket

An **ice bucket** is a simple container used to hold ice and keep samples or reagents cold during experiments.

Ice buckets are very common in wet lab work because many enzymes, nucleic acids, proteins, and biological samples are temperature-sensitive. Keeping them on ice can slow degradation and help maintain activity or stability.

Ice buckets are commonly used for:

- Keeping enzymes cold
- Keeping DNA/RNA samples cold
- Keeping protein samples cold
- Holding PCR reagents before setup
- Maintaining temperature-sensitive samples during extraction
- Temporarily storing tubes during an experiment

For example, during PCR setup, enzyme mixes or DNA polymerase are often kept on ice before being placed into the PCR machine. During RNA extraction, samples and reagents may also be kept cold to reduce RNA degradation.

The temperature of an ice bucket is usually around **0°C**, depending on the ice/water mixture. It is much warmer than -20°C or liquid nitrogen, so it is mainly used for short-term cooling during active experimental work.

Important points:

- Do not let tube labels get damaged by melted ice water.
- Make sure tubes are closed tightly to avoid water contamination.
- Use a tube rack or floating rack if needed.
- Replace ice when it melts.
- Keep ice buckets clean to avoid contamination.
- For RNA or protein work, keeping samples cold can be very important.

The main purpose of an ice bucket is:

> To keep samples and reagents cold during short-term experimental handling.

---

### 1.6 Cooling Block / Cold Block

A **cooling block**, also called a **cold block**, is a metal or temperature-conductive block used to keep tubes cold in a more organized and stable way than placing tubes directly into ice.

This is sometimes informally described as a “cold metal block,” but in most labs it is not called an iron block. A more accurate name is usually **cooling block**, **cold block**, or **aluminum cooling block**.

Cooling blocks usually have holes designed for specific tube sizes, such as:

- PCR tubes
- 0.2 mL tubes
- 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes
- 2 mL tubes
- Plate strips

Cooling blocks can be pre-chilled on ice, in a refrigerator, in a freezer, or sometimes on dry ice, depending on the experiment. Once chilled, they help keep samples at a low temperature while also holding tubes upright and organized.

Cooling blocks are commonly used for:

- PCR setup
- Enzyme reaction setup
- RNA work
- Protein work
- Keeping samples cold without direct contact with melting ice
- Organizing tubes while maintaining low temperature

Compared with placing tubes directly in ice, cooling blocks have several advantages:

- Tubes stay upright and organized.
- Labels are less likely to get wet.
- There is less risk of ice water entering tubes.
- Temperature contact is more consistent.
- It is easier to handle multiple samples in order.

For example, during PCR setup, a cooling block can hold PCR tubes or strip tubes while keeping master mix, primers, and template DNA cold. This helps reduce non-specific reactions before the PCR program starts.

Important points:

- Pre-chill the block before use.
- Choose the correct block for the tube size.
- Do not assume the block stays cold forever; it gradually warms up at room temperature.
- For very temperature-sensitive samples, monitor time carefully.
- Keep the block clean to avoid contamination.

The main purpose of a cooling block is:

> To keep tubes cold, upright, and organized during temperature-sensitive experimental steps.

## 2. Safety Equipment: Fume Hood

A **fume hood** is a safety device used when handling volatile, toxic, hazardous, or irritating chemicals. It protects users by using airflow to pull harmful vapors, gases, or fumes away from the working area.

Fume hoods are commonly used when working with:

- Organic solvents
- Toxic chemicals
- Strong acids or bases
- Chemicals that produce harmful vapors
- Procedures that may release hazardous fumes

It is important to understand that a fume hood is mainly designed for **chemical safety**, not for maintaining a sterile environment.

A fume hood is different from a **biosafety cabinet**. A biosafety cabinet is commonly used in cell culture or microbiology experiments to protect the sample, the user, and the environment from biological contamination. In contrast, a fume hood mainly protects the user from harmful chemical vapors.

When using a fume hood:

- Keep the sash at the proper working height.
- Do not block airflow inside the hood.
- Do not use the fume hood as a long-term storage space.
- Make sure the hood is functioning before starting work.
- Handle hazardous chemicals carefully and follow lab safety rules.

---

### 2.1 Hazardous Waste Container

Some fume hoods contain a **hazardous waste container** for collecting chemical waste.

Hazardous waste may include:

- Organic solvent waste
- Toxic chemical waste
- Phenol/chloroform waste
- Chemical mixtures that cannot be poured into the sink
- Contaminated liquid waste from specific protocols

The purpose of a hazardous waste container is to prevent dangerous chemicals from being disposed of incorrectly. Many chemical wastes cannot go into the sink or regular trash because they may be toxic, flammable, corrosive, or harmful to the environment.

Important points:

- Always check the waste label before adding anything.
- Do not mix incompatible chemicals.
- Keep the waste container closed when not in use.
- Do not overfill the container.
- If unsure, ask a lab member or follow institutional safety guidelines.

Proper waste disposal is part of experimental safety and should be treated as seriously as the experiment itself.

## 3. Liquid Handling and Sample Organization Tools

Liquid handling is one of the most fundamental skills in wet lab work. Whether preparing buffers, loading samples, setting up PCR reactions, or working with cells, accurate liquid transfer is essential.

In addition to pipettes, simple tools such as tube racks are also important because they help keep samples organized and reduce the risk of mistakes.

---

### 3.1 Rack

A **rack** is used to hold, organize, and stabilize tubes during experiments. It does not directly participate in a reaction or change the sample itself, but it helps make the workflow more organized and safer.

Common racks can hold:

- 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes
- 2 mL tubes
- 5 mL tubes
- 15 mL conical tubes
- 50 mL conical tubes
- PCR tubes
- PCR strips

The main purpose of a rack is to keep tubes upright and organized. This helps prevent spills, sample mix-ups, and cross-contamination.

For example, during DNA extraction or PCR setup, samples are often placed in a rack according to their sample ID. This makes it easier to follow the correct order and reduces the risk of adding the wrong reagent to the wrong tube.

Important points:

- Choose the correct rack for the tube type.
- Keep sample order clear.
- Label tubes properly.
- Avoid placing open tubes too close to each other.
- Use a cold rack or ice box if samples need to stay cold.

In simple terms, the function of a rack is:

> To organize and hold tubes during experimental procedures.

---

### 3.2 Pipette Gun

A **pipette gun**, also called a **pipette controller**, is used with serological pipettes to transfer relatively large volumes of liquid, usually at the milliliter scale.

It is commonly used for:

- Transferring culture media
- Adding or removing buffers
- Handling large volumes of solution
- Cell culture work
- Preparing reagents or solutions

Common serological pipette volumes include:

- 1 mL
- 5 mL
- 10 mL
- 25 mL
- 50 mL

A pipette gun is useful when the volume is too large for a micropipette.

---

### 3.3 Micropipette

A **micropipette** is used to transfer small volumes of liquid, usually at the microliter scale.

Common micropipettes include:

- **P10**: usually for 0.5–10 µL
- **P20**: usually for 2–20 µL
- **P200**: usually for 20–200 µL
- **P1000**: usually for 100–1000 µL

Micropipettes are commonly used for:

- PCR setup
- DNA/RNA extraction
- Enzyme reactions
- Loading gels
- Adding small volumes of reagents
- Preparing small-volume samples

The difference between a pipette gun and a micropipette is mainly the volume range:

| Tool | Typical Volume Range | Common Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Pipette gun | mL scale | Media, buffers, large-volume solutions |
| Micropipette | µL scale | PCR, DNA/RNA work, enzyme reactions |

Accurate pipetting is extremely important because small volume errors can affect concentration, reaction efficiency, and final results.

---

### 3.4 Multi-Channel Pipette

A **multi-channel pipette** is a pipette that can aspirate and dispense multiple liquid volumes at the same time. The most common types are **8-channel** and **12-channel** pipettes, which are often used with 96-well plates.

The purpose of a multi-channel pipette is to increase liquid handling efficiency and reduce the time and human error caused by repetitive pipetting.

Multi-channel pipettes are commonly used for:

- PCR plate setup
- qPCR
- ELISA
- Cell-based assays
- Serial dilution
- Plate washing
- Adding master mix
- Transferring samples between plates

For example, if we need to add the same volume of master mix to every well of a 96-well plate, using a single-channel micropipette would be slow and more error-prone. A multi-channel pipette allows us to handle an entire row of wells at once, making the process faster and more consistent.

Important points:

- Make sure every tip is attached firmly.
- Make sure all tips contact the liquid when aspirating.
- Watch the plate orientation carefully.
- Avoid adding samples to the wrong row or column.
- Check for bubbles or uneven liquid levels.

For plate-based experiments, orientation is extremely important. Once sample positions are mixed up, downstream analysis can become very confusing.

---

### 3.5 Pipette Tips and Tip Boxes

**Pipette tips** are disposable plastic tips attached to micropipettes for accurate liquid transfer. **Tip boxes** are used to store and organize pipette tips.

Common types of tips include:

- Standard tips
- Filter tips
- Sterile tips
- Low-retention tips

For contamination-sensitive experiments such as PCR, qPCR, RNA work, or sequencing library preparation, **filter tips** are commonly used. Filter tips contain a small barrier that helps reduce aerosol contamination and cross-contamination between samples.

Important pipetting habits:

- Change tips between different samples.
- Use filter tips for sensitive experiments.
- Avoid touching the inside of tubes with tips when possible.
- Make sure the tip is properly attached to the pipette.
- Do not reuse tips unless the protocol specifically allows it.

Even a very small amount of contamination can affect experimental results, especially in PCR-based experiments.

---

### 3.6 Opentrons

**Opentrons** is a laboratory automation platform, also known as an **automated liquid handling robot**. It can perform pipetting steps automatically based on a programmed protocol.

It can be used for:

- Aspirating liquid
- Dispensing liquid
- Mixing samples
- Transferring samples
- Distributing reagents
- Setting up plate-based experiments

In wet lab work, Opentrons is commonly used for workflows that are repetitive, high-throughput, or sensitive to human error.

Examples include:

- PCR setup
- qPCR plate preparation
- DNA/RNA cleanup
- Sequencing library preparation
- Serial dilution
- Sample normalization
- Plate-based assays

The main advantage of Opentrons is that it improves consistency and reproducibility. Manual pipetting can be affected by human technique, fatigue, and small differences in operation. In contrast, an automated liquid handler can repeat the same action using the same programmed parameters.

Another advantage is time saving. For experiments involving 96-well plates or many samples, Opentrons can reduce manual workload and allow researchers to focus more on experimental design, result checking, and troubleshooting.

However, Opentrons is not simply a machine where we can put in a protocol and expect everything to work automatically. Before running a protocol, we need to confirm that the setup is correct, including:

- Tips
- Plates
- Tubes
- Reservoirs
- Deck position
- Labware definitions
- Liquid volumes
- Pipetting height
- Mixing steps
- Tip change strategy
- Liquid type

If the setup is wrong, the robot may repeat the wrong action consistently. Therefore, understanding the protocol is still very important.

Automation does not replace experimental understanding. It helps us execute experiments more consistently.

## 4. Sample Preparation and Separation Equipment

Many biological samples need to be disrupted, ground, centrifuged, separated, or purified before DNA, RNA, or protein extraction. These sample preparation steps are important because they affect extraction efficiency and final sample quality.

---

### 4.1 Automill / Tissue Homogenizer

An **automill**, or **tissue homogenizer**, is used to grind and homogenize solid or semi-solid biological samples.

It can break down tissue and make the sample more uniform, allowing lysis buffer to contact the sample more effectively during extraction.

It is commonly used for:

- Plant tissue
- Animal tissue
- Bacterial pellets
- Solid or semi-solid biological samples
- Samples prepared for DNA, RNA, or protein extraction

For example, in plant DNA extraction, leaf tissue often needs to be thoroughly ground before lysis buffer is added. This helps release DNA from the cells.

Compared with manual grinding, an automill is usually:

- Faster
- More consistent
- Better for processing multiple samples
- Less dependent on individual technique

Proper homogenization can strongly affect extraction efficiency and final sample quality.

---

### 4.2 Stainless Steel Beads / Grinding Beads

**Stainless steel beads**, also called **grinding beads** or **homogenization beads**, are small metal beads used to help physically disrupt and homogenize biological samples.

They are commonly used together with equipment such as an **automill**, **tissue homogenizer**, or **bead mill**. During homogenization, the machine shakes or moves the sample tubes rapidly, and the beads hit the tissue or pellet repeatedly. This mechanical force helps break the sample into smaller pieces and makes it easier to extract DNA, RNA, proteins, or other molecules.

Stainless steel beads are especially useful for tough or fibrous samples that are difficult to break by simple pipetting or vortexing.

They are commonly used for:

- Plant tissue grinding
- Animal tissue homogenization
- Tough cell disruption
- Bacterial or fungal sample disruption
- DNA extraction
- RNA extraction
- Protein extraction
- Metabolite extraction

For example, in plant DNA extraction, leaf tissue can be placed into a tube with stainless steel beads and lysis buffer, or the tissue can be kept frozen before grinding. When the tube is processed in an automill, the beads help crush the tissue into a more uniform mixture. This allows the lysis buffer to contact the cells more efficiently and improves extraction yield.

The main function of stainless steel beads is:

> To mechanically break and homogenize biological samples during sample preparation.

Compared with manual grinding, using stainless steel beads with a homogenizer can be:

- Faster
- More consistent
- Better for processing many samples
- More effective for tough tissues
- Less dependent on individual technique

However, stainless steel beads should be used carefully. Because they generate strong mechanical force, over-grinding may damage sensitive molecules or structures.

Important points:

- Use the correct bead size for the sample and tube.
- Make sure tubes are compatible with bead beating or homogenization.
- Do not overfill tubes.
- Ensure tubes are tightly closed before homogenization.
- Avoid excessive grinding if working with long genomic DNA, fragile nuclei, or sensitive protein complexes.
- Clean, sterilize, or use new beads when required to prevent cross-contamination.
- Be careful when removing beads after grinding.

For high-molecular-weight DNA extraction, excessive bead beating may cause **DNA shearing**. For nuclei isolation, strong homogenization may damage nuclei. Therefore, bead size, grinding time, shaking speed, and sample type all need to be considered.

Stainless steel beads are different from magnetic beads:

| Bead Type | Main Purpose | Common Use |
|---|---|---|
| Stainless steel beads / grinding beads | Physically break and homogenize samples | Tissue grinding, sample disruption |
| Magnetic beads | Bind and purify target molecules | DNA/RNA cleanup, protein pull-down, library preparation |

In simple terms:

- **Stainless steel beads** are used to break samples.
- **Magnetic beads** are used to bind and purify molecules.

---

### 4.3 Centrifuge

A **centrifuge** is used to separate components in a sample by spinning at high speed and generating centrifugal force.

In simple terms, a centrifuge can force certain components to the bottom of a tube, forming a **pellet**, while other components remain in the liquid phase, called the **supernatant**.

Centrifuges are commonly used for:

- DNA extraction
- RNA extraction
- Protein extraction
- Cell collection
- Bacterial pelleting
- Sample clarification
- Purification steps

For example, in DNA extraction, centrifugation can be used to pellet cell debris or precipitated material, allowing us to collect the supernatant. It can also be used after DNA precipitation to collect DNA as a pellet at the bottom of the tube.

In cell-related experiments, centrifugation is often used to collect cell pellets, remove old medium, or perform washing steps.

Common types of centrifuges include:

- **Microcentrifuge**: commonly used for 1.5 mL or 2 mL tubes.
- **Bench-top centrifuge**: can handle larger tubes or plates.
- **Refrigerated centrifuge**: can spin samples at low temperature, which is useful for temperature-sensitive samples such as RNA, proteins, or cells.

One very important rule when using a centrifuge is **balance**. Before spinning, the rotor must be balanced. If the number of tubes is odd, a balance tube with the same volume of liquid is usually needed.

Improper balancing can cause:

- Strong vibration
- Sample damage
- Rotor damage
- Damage to the centrifuge
- Safety risks

In protocols, centrifugation conditions may be written as **rpm** or **×g / RCF**. Strictly speaking, **×g / RCF** is more accurate because different rotors have different radii. The same rpm can produce different centrifugal forces depending on the rotor.

---

### 4.4 Magnetic Rack

A **magnetic rack** is a tube rack with magnets. It is mainly used for **magnetic bead-based experiments**.

Its function is to use magnetic force to pull magnetic beads to the side of a tube or plate, allowing the beads to be separated from the liquid phase.

Magnetic racks are commonly used for:

- DNA cleanup
- RNA cleanup
- Sequencing library preparation
- Bead-based purification
- Protein pull-down
- Nuclei isolation workflows
- Immunoprecipitation-related experiments

In many experiments, target molecules bind to magnetic beads. For example, in DNA cleanup, DNA can bind to bead surfaces under specific buffer conditions. After the tube is placed on a magnetic rack, the beads are pulled to the side of the tube and the liquid becomes clear. At this point, the supernatant can be carefully removed, wash buffer can be added, and finally the target DNA can be eluted using elution buffer.

The core function of a magnetic rack is:

> To separate magnetic beads from the liquid phase without centrifugation.

The difference between a regular rack and a magnetic rack is:

| Tool | Main Function |
|---|---|
| Rack | Holds and organizes tubes |
| Magnetic rack | Holds tubes and pulls magnetic beads to the side using magnets |

Important points:

- Wait until beads are fully attracted to the magnet before removing liquid.
- Avoid touching the bead pellet with the pipette tip.
- Do not accidentally aspirate beads.
- Make sure beads are fully resuspended during washing or elution.
- During elution, allow beads and elution buffer to interact properly.
- Use the correct magnetic rack for the tube or plate type.

Although a magnetic rack looks simple, it is very important in bead-based purification. If beads are not fully captured, or if beads are accidentally removed during pipetting, DNA, RNA, or protein yield may be reduced.

## 5. Incubation, Mixing, and Temperature-Controlled Equipment

Many experimental reactions need to happen at a specific temperature. Some reactions also require mixing so that samples and reagents can interact evenly. Therefore, incubation, mixing, and temperature-controlled equipment are very common in wet lab work.

---

### 5.1 Incubator

An **incubator** provides a stable environment for biological samples.

Depending on the type, an incubator may control:

- Temperature
- Humidity
- CO₂ concentration
- Shaking speed

A regular incubator is often used for:

- Growing bacteria
- Incubating agar plates
- Maintaining samples at a fixed temperature

A **CO₂ incubator** is commonly used for mammalian cell culture. In cell culture, CO₂ helps maintain the pH of the culture medium through the CO₂-bicarbonate buffer system.

The main purpose of an incubator is to keep samples under suitable conditions for growth, reaction, or incubation.

---

### 5.2 Water Bath

A **water bath** uses heated water to maintain a stable temperature.

Because water surrounds the sample tube, a water bath can provide relatively even and stable heat transfer.

It is commonly used for:

- Warming reagents
- Gently thawing samples
- Incubating enzyme reactions
- Heat treatment steps
- Maintaining samples at a specific temperature

When using a water bath, make sure tubes are properly closed to prevent water from entering the sample. If the sample is sensitive to contamination, the cleanliness of the water bath should also be considered.

---

### 5.3 Vortexer

A **vortexer** is a small instrument used to rapidly mix liquid samples. It shakes tubes at high speed, causing the liquid inside to form a vortex.

Vortexers are commonly used for:

- Mixing reagents
- Resuspending pellets
- Mixing DNA/RNA samples
- Mixing protein samples
- Resuspending magnetic beads
- Mixing bacterial cultures
- Preparing extraction reactions

For example, during DNA extraction, samples may need to be vortexed after lysis buffer is added so that the sample and buffer can mix thoroughly.

In magnetic bead cleanup, vortexing or pipette mixing may be used after adding beads to make sure the beads are evenly suspended.

The main function of a vortexer is:

> To rapidly mix samples by mechanical shaking.

A vortexer is different from a gentle mixer. Its mixing force is relatively strong, which is useful for rapid and complete mixing. However, some samples are sensitive to mechanical force and should not be vortexed too aggressively.

Samples that require caution include:

- Long genomic DNA
- Fragile cells
- Nuclei
- Some protein complexes
- Beads that should be gently mixed
- Samples sensitive to mechanical shearing

For example, if the goal is to extract high-molecular-weight genomic DNA, excessive vortexing may shear the DNA and break long fragments. If working with nuclei isolation samples, strong vortexing may damage nuclei structure.

Important points:

- Make sure tube caps are closed tightly.
- Choose appropriate vortex strength and duration.
- Avoid over-vortexing mechanically sensitive samples.
- Keep vortexing conditions consistent across samples.
- If gentle mixing is needed, consider using a tube rotator, rocker, or pipette mixing instead.

---

### 5.4 Thermomixer

A **thermomixer** combines temperature control with shaking or mixing.

It can heat or cool samples while continuously mixing them.

It is commonly used for:

- DNA/RNA extraction
- Enzyme reactions
- Protein digestion
- Bead-based reactions
- Small-volume reactions that require both temperature control and mixing

The advantage of a thermomixer is that it keeps the sample at a stable temperature while preventing uneven reactions, precipitation, or poor reagent mixing.

---

### 5.5 HulaMixer / Tube Rotator

A **HulaMixer**, or **tube rotator**, is used for gentle continuous mixing by rotating tubes.

It is commonly used for:

- Antibody incubation
- Protein binding reactions
- Bead-based purification
- Gentle mixing of biological samples

Compared with vortexing, a HulaMixer provides much gentler mixing. It is suitable for reactions that should not be mixed too aggressively, such as antibody binding or bead-target interactions.

---

### 5.6 Thermo Rocker

A **thermo rocker** provides a gentle rocking motion. Some models also provide temperature control.

It allows liquid to move back and forth inside a tube, plate, or container, helping reagents contact the sample more evenly.

It is commonly used for:

- Membrane incubation
- Antibody staining
- Washing steps
- Bead reactions
- Plate-based incubation

The rocking motion is gentle and suitable for long incubation or washing steps. For example, in Western blot experiments, antibody incubation and washing steps are often performed on a rocker.

---

## 6. DNA/RNA Experiment Equipment

In molecular biology, some machines are specifically used for DNA or RNA work, such as PCR machines and gel electrophoresis systems.

---

### 6.1 PCR Machine / Thermal Cycler

A **PCR machine**, also called a **thermal cycler**, is used to amplify specific DNA fragments through polymerase chain reaction.

The machine repeatedly changes temperature to allow DNA to go through three major steps:

1. **Denaturation**: double-stranded DNA separates into single strands.
2. **Annealing**: primers bind to the target DNA sequence.
3. **Extension**: DNA polymerase synthesizes new DNA strands.

PCR machines are commonly used for:

- Amplifying target DNA
- Genotyping
- Colony PCR
- Checking cloning results
- Preparing DNA fragments for sequencing or downstream analysis

PCR is one of the core techniques in molecular biology because it can amplify a very small amount of DNA into a detectable and analyzable amount.

---

### 6.2 Gel Runner / Gel Electrophoresis System

A **gel runner**, or **gel electrophoresis system**, is used to separate DNA, RNA, or proteins based on size.

For DNA agarose gel electrophoresis, the system usually includes:

- Gel tank
- Gel tray
- Comb
- Running buffer
- Power supply

DNA is negatively charged, so it moves toward the positive electrode in an electric field. Agarose gel acts like a molecular sieve. Smaller DNA fragments move faster, while larger fragments move more slowly. This allows DNA fragments of different sizes to be separated.

Gel electrophoresis is commonly used for:

- Checking PCR products
- Confirming DNA extraction quality
- Verifying restriction digestion
- Separating DNA fragments for gel purification

---

## 7. Quantification, Imaging, and Detection Equipment

After experiments, we often need to know the concentration, purity, size, quantity, or visual result of a sample. Therefore, detection and quantification equipment are also essential in wet lab work.

---

### 7.1 Nanodrop

A **Nanodrop** is a microvolume spectrophotometer commonly used to quickly measure the concentration and purity of DNA, RNA, or proteins.

It requires only a small amount of sample, usually around 1–2 µL.

For nucleic acids, Nanodrop provides values such as:

- Concentration
- A260/280 ratio
- A260/230 ratio

These values help estimate nucleic acid concentration and evaluate possible contamination from proteins, salts, organic solvents, or other impurities.

Advantages of Nanodrop:

- Fast
- Convenient
- Requires very little sample
- Provides purity ratios

Limitations of Nanodrop:

- May overestimate concentration
- Cannot always distinguish target nucleic acids from contaminants
- Less accurate for low-concentration samples

Nanodrop is useful for a quick quality check, but it may not always provide the most accurate concentration.

---

### 7.2 Qubit

A **Qubit** is a fluorescence-based instrument used to measure the concentration of DNA, RNA, or proteins.

Unlike Nanodrop, Qubit uses fluorescent dyes that bind specifically to the target molecule. This makes Qubit more accurate, especially for low-concentration samples.

Qubit is commonly used for:

- Accurate DNA quantification
- Accurate RNA quantification
- Sequencing sample preparation
- Library preparation
- Low-concentration nucleic acid measurement

In simple terms:

- **Nanodrop** is better for quick concentration and purity checks.
- **Qubit** is better for accurate quantification.

---

### 7.3 UV Light Detection Machine / Gel Imaging System

A **UV light detection machine**, also called a **UV transilluminator** or **gel imaging system**, is used to visualize DNA or RNA bands in agarose gels.

After DNA or RNA binds to a nucleic acid stain, it can fluoresce under UV or blue light. The gel imaging system allows us to see and record the position, size, and approximate intensity of DNA bands.

It is commonly used for:

- Checking PCR results
- Confirming DNA fragment size
- Recording gel electrophoresis results
- Cutting DNA bands for gel extraction

Safety note:

UV light can damage the eyes and skin. When using UV equipment, always use a protective shield, protective glasses, or other appropriate safety measures.

### 7.4 Fluorescence Cell Counter

A **fluorescence cell counter** is used to count cells and evaluate cell status. It usually uses fluorescent dyes to identify cells, and in some cases, it can distinguish live cells from dead cells.

It is commonly used for:

- Cell culture
- Transfection preparation
- Flow cytometry sample preparation
- Cell concentration measurement
- Cell viability assessment
- Nuclei isolation or nuclei extraction workflows

Compared with manual cell counting, a fluorescence cell counter is usually:

- Faster
- More standardized
- Less affected by human error
- Useful for viability-based measurements

In our context, it may also be used during **nuclei isolation/extraction** to evaluate nuclei concentration and sample quality.
